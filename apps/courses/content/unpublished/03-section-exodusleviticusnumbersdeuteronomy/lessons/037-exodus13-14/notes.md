**Exodus 13:11-16 > You owe your firstborn to Yahweh**

- INCLUSIOS WITHIN INCLUSIOS: A complex double inclusio started in chapter 12 when we broke the action to tell about the passover and the feast of unleavened bread. Then in the middle we got the actual death of the firstborn, then we concluded with the passover and the feast of unleavened bread again, and that inclusio is finished with its own firstborn consecration inclusio. The center of the inclusio is the death of the firstborn, and the final inclusio is that you owe God every firstborn as a result but they can be redeemed. It’s all about the death of the firstborn and its memory. There is also a death of the firstborn inclusio that started when God first told Moses in the wilderness that he would kill Pharaoh’s firstborn, and it completes when the firstborn die, and then echoes through the redemption of the firstborn inclusio. So beautifully complex.
- CONSECRATE AND REDEEM DEFINITION: “redeem” comes from Latin “redimure”, “re” meaning “again” or “back” and “dimure” meaning purchase. It means to buy back. So a first purchase is not a redeem. Redeem is to buy something back that you lost or already had. Consecrate is “con” (meaning with) “secrate” is from “secrare" meaning “sacred” or “holy”. So it means “with sacredness”. It is a dedication of something to be used in God’s service.
- CONSECRATE FIRST BORN: you don’t get to fully keep your first born. They belong to God. For animals, this means they must die on this Earth, (at least) symbolically to then be with God. The first born were to perform the religious duties for God, but God changes this requirement in Numbers 3:11-13 to just be Levites, and fixes the firstborn redemption price at 5 shekels in Numbers 18.
  - WHY DOES ISRAEL OWE HIM THEIR FIRSTBORN? As Mike Winger put it, God is saying “Israel, you owe me a firstborn.” Why do they owe their firstborn forever? God’s justice demands it for sin because he punished some sinners (Egypt) and not others (Israel). **The firstborn of Egypt had to die for their freedom.** So Israel forever owes Yahweh their firstborn.
  - REDEEM OPTION: But God does not want humans to kill all their firstborn even though justice demands it. So he offers them an out: someone can redeem you. **Someone else can buy the firstborn back from God in lieu of him or her actually having to die or spend their life serving God in religious duties overseeing the tabernacle or temple.** The firstborn animals were sacrificed, and a lamb can take the place in death as a sacrifice for an unclean animal like a donkey.
  - GOD IS NOT EXEMPT FROM HIS OWN LAW: he also gives his firstborn son to Yahweh, just as all the Israelites have done since the Exodus. But we didn’t give him the option to buy back his son for 5 shekels. **We didn’t say “ok, we’ll stop sinning, he doesn’t need to die. Just give us 5 shekels and we’ll stop sinning.**” No, he had to go through with it all the way to death. But now God’s household, of which we became a part when we repented and trusted in Jesus, has its firstborn already consecrated to Yahweh. Because of Jesus, we no longer owe firstborns to God. Instead, we just owe him everything.

**Exodus 16:4-8 > Yahweh says Meat and Manna will test the people**

- WHY NOT TELL THE PEOPLE IT IS A TEST? Yahweh tells Moses, but he doesn’t tell the people being tested. Turns out, if you tell a test subject they are being tested, it can change the outcome. If I tell you were are going to study whether blue or green colored soap in the bathrooms causes more handwashing, and we put the regular soap in one bathroom as a control, blue in another, and green in another, well most likely find all soap use and hand washing increased, especially if there were a camera at the sink or a human observer taking notes. Telling people about a test in advance can affect the test (but not always predictably). Just as Pharaoh had to have his heart hardened so he could exercise his free will in the face of plagues, God doesn’t want to influence their free will by telling them they are being tested, so he just tells Moses.
- WHAT IS THE TEST? The most obvious test is will they do what God says or not? Will they adhere to his law or not? Will they try to stock up manna? Will they try to gather some on the Sabbath? But the commentators suggest there are two other tests that at first seem opposite each other, and yet in God’s genius, he achievedd them both with the same action. First, God is testing their trust in him. Are they willing to trust God that every single day the food they need will be there? In other words, in their poverty, will they trust God every day to pull them through? But the second test is that once they do trust God to pull them through every day, they no longer need to worry about their food and their survival. They will have become affluent. And in their affluence, will they fall away from God, losing that deep feeling of need for him? Dennis Prager points out this means that God tests us both in our poverty, and in our affluence. God test, poor, and God tests the rich. The rich think “I’m so successful, who needs God“, and the poor think there is no God. And the poor thing “if I were rich, then I would believe in God“, and think “if I were poor, then I would believe in God“, but they are both wrong.
  - But wait, if God tests both before and the rich, didn’t Jesus say it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of heaven? Doesn’t that mean he tested the rich more? Isn’t the obvious take away from Jesus teaching that poor people go to heaven and rich people go to hell? So to get to heaven, we need to sell everything we have in voluntarily live in poverty? A lot of people think that. But if you look at what Jesus actually said, he didn’t say anything about poor people. He said it’s hard for rich people to get in heaven. And that is true, nothing draws Israel, toward other gods more than affluence. The rich don’t seek God because they have what they need in this life.
  - And of course, the disciples ask Jesus, “Lord than who can be saved?” it was assumed in that day that if you were rich, it was because God blessed you. If even they can’t make it into heaven, then what hope do the rest of us have?
  - What Jesus said was completely true and appropriate in context. But the part left out of that statement is the poor don’t make it into heaven either. Who goes to heaven? Good people. You could have good poor people, and you could have good rich people. But the trouble is, you have no good people at all. Nobody is good, so nobody gets to heaven, rich or poor. So heaven is empty. The only people going there are the ones to whom the goodness of Jesus washes away their sin because they have repented of their sins and chosen to trust him. Rich and poor. Slave and free.
  - Paul addresses this in the New Testament, Philippians 4:11-13 where he attests “Not that I am speaking of being in need, for I have learned in whatever situation I am to be content. I know how to be brought low, and I know how to abound. In any and every circumstance, I have learned the secret of facing plenty and hunger, abundance and need. I can do all things through him who strengthens me.”
  - God started to reveal this truth to us, that the poor and the rich both need God, they both experience tests of faith, way back here in the desert in Exodus.
