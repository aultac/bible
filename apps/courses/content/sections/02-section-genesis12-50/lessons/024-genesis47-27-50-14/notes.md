His fear drove him to create a self fulfilling prophecy.. had he not acted to protect Egypt from the Hebrews in the 1st place, Moses never would have ended up with his daughter. Has Dennis Prager points out, his daughter is a testament to the fact that just because your parents are evil does not mean you have to be. You can be the pharaoh’s daughter..

**Exodus 2:11-15 > Moses kills Egyptian**

- MOSES WAS OF THE TRIBE OF LEVI: Jacob said Levi and Simeon’s anger was devastating. Moses seems to have inherited his ancestor’s anger at unrighteousness.
- WAS HIS KILLING OF THE EGYPTIAN IMMORAL? This is a hard question. We weren’t there, we don’t know what the Egyptian was doing. I come down on the side of it was not legal, but it was moral. And if you don’t agree, it is probably because you have never witnessed the pure evil of a man mercilessly whipping another human being purely to torture them. Every crack. Every stroke. Blood. Pain. Terror. Despair. Cries for mercy from the depth of the soul. Watch the movie “12 Years a Slave” and then tell me you don’t wish someone, anyone would come along and take out the evil man with the whip. Watch any videos of how Muslim men treat their wives and female slaves in Muslim countries, and tell me you don’t wish for a savior to save the woman and kill the abuser. Be very wary of judging those who fight evil as one who has never experienced it. Moses’ killing of the Egyptian is not like killing an entire town for the sins of one man like his ancestor Levi did. The Egyptian died for his own actions, even though his life and job were precisely to perform that sort of action. God seems to approve of Moses’ actions, he chooses Moses to be the greatest Israelite until one day, as Moses himself tells us in Deuteronomy 18:15: "The LORD your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among you, from your brothers-it is to him you shall listen.” Moses’ reaction to this event is one of the great testaments to his character: he hates evil so much he is willing to risk his own life of luxury purely to stop it.
- AMBIGUOUS MORALITY WITHOUT GOD’S LAW: the Hebrew’s taunt to Moses is jarring to him, and to us. How could the people he helped betray him like that? Didn’t they see how wrong the Egyptian was? This is the unmooring of morality from God we talked about earlier. The Nietzschean superman of Pharaoh made it hard to see the objective good and evil. But Moses, great man that he was, saw it. And he not only saw it, he had the courage to act on it, driven by a hatred of that objective evil and injustice. Moses, the anti-Pharaoh.
- WE NEED GOD’S LAW: are we not still begging God to give his law, to settle the question of good and evil? To tell Moses he was right and tell the Egyptian slave driver he was wrong?
- NATURAL REACTION OF THE GUILTY: Dennis Prager points out that the guilty man’s reaction is normal: he doesn’t address Moses’s question, he knows he is guilty. Instead, he attacks Moses’s own character like “you’re no better than me.” Is actually a complete non-Sequitur to talk about Moses’s past. The correct answer is “I was wrong and I should do better.” The other man can also do better, but the fact that he doesn’t do better is irrelevant to whether you should do better. In your own life, when someone accuses you of something, make sure your first response is not jumping to the sins of your accuser but rather first asking yourself is it true?

**Exodus 2:16-22 > Moses in Midian**

- MOSES’ CHARACTER: we see 3 actions by Moses that introduce us to his character. 1: killing the Egyptian torturer, 2: confronting the Hebrew abuser, and 3: Saving the Midianite girls and wandering their flock. You could have thought with the first one that perhaps he was just angered by violence toward his own group, but now we see the kind of man that Moses is. He hates injustice, to the point of taking action every time. It is this man God chose to bring the law to Israel.
- FATHER OF SEVEN DAUGHTERS: the man with seven unmarried daughters is obviously eager for the kind strong Egyptian to come back to the house.
- ZIPPORAH AND MOSES: this was not portrayed as a love story like Rebecca and Isaac, or a tragic love story like Jacob and Rachel. She was simply given to him in marriage, she can conceived, and later they have a sort of strange argument about circumcision. Clearly, this story was written by a man. Just the facts, none of the romance. Moses is portrayed as a good man, not a romantic or desirable man. He doesn’t seem to turn out to be a great husband or a great father. But he is a great man. He stood on the mountain top with Jesus when he was transfigured.

**Exodus 2:23-25 > God saw and God Knew**

- ANTHROPOMORPHIZING GOD: as we talked about one of our earlier classes, when we use the language of human emotions to apply to God, it helps us relate but it sacrifices accuracy. God remembers, but he does not forget. God knew, but he there was no time before that he didn’t know. Got heard, but it’s not like he didn’t hear their earlier cries.
- WHY DOES GOD CHOOSE TO INTERVENE AT SOME TIMES AND NOT OTHERS? Why did God rescue the Jews here, but not from the Holocaust? So many innocent people have suffered so much in this world, dying, torturous deaths and obscurity, for no good reason that we can see. The people who hurt them never paid a price.
  - This question is known as Theodicy. It is THE question. As the Rabbi Milton Steinberg pointed out, the religious person has to account for the existence of unjust suffering. The atheist has to account for the existence of everything else.
  - Our Western movies have filled our heads with underdogs who win, bad guys who lose in the end, victims who get justice. To the point that we just expect it. And our movies should be like that. Rooting for the good guy is intrinsically Christianity to its core. Those of you who love God must hate evil.
  - The sad truth is that In reality that rarely happens. The bad guy more often wins, the bad guy more often never pays a price, the victim How can God be good when so much evil exists, and he has the power to stop it?
  - Read Habbakuk
  - Read C S Lewis monologue born in pain die in pain.
  - This question
  - In most cases, we have no idea why he intervenes or even when he is intervening. God is his own agent and often keeps his own counsel. If he tells us why he is doing something, then we know, otherwise we are just guessing.
  - In this case, we do know: we can see how the plan of salvation unfolded later to see why this was necessary, and he tells us that he didn’t pull them out of Egypt until the iniquity of the Amorites was complete. He waited until they were like the people before the flood: all their thoughts were constantly bent on evil all the time, until there was nothing good or redeemable left in that land at all.
  - But most of life we don’t really know. We just have to trust him. Because that’s what life is all about, trusting in the good God who created the universe. Will you trust him even if the problem you have doesn’t get fixed?
