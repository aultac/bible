**Exodus 1:17-end > Midwives**

- MIDWIVES WERE EGYPTIAN: The Hebrew can equally mean midwives who were Hebrew or the midwives of the Hebrews. The story context seems to be pretty clear they are not Hebrews, though.
- SUPERHEROES WITHOUT POWERS: we are used to seeing a savior swoop in to save the day in our western age of entertainment. We see it so much that we expected it happen all the time. The human is hardwired to look for a savior. **But these midwives aren’t wearing an Iron Man suit**. They have no super strength, they don’t shoot lasers out of their eyes, they can’t fly. **But they have superhuman moral courage.** To directly disobey the order of the pharaoh, and then get called to his palace to stand before him, and to leave that meeting unscathed: **the God they feared was with them.** May we all aspire to have 1/10 the courage of Shiphrah and Puah. Perhaps instead of blessing our children as may you be as Ephraim and Manasseh, we should instead **bless them that they be as Shiphrah and Puah who refused to be a cog in the baby killing machine of the state.**
- WERE THE MIDWIVES RIGHT TO LIE? Yes. Lying to save the lives of infants was a fundamentally good act. We talked about this two weeks ago: absolute morality is absolutely situational. The rightness/wrongness of an act depends on the circumstances. **Your proof of this is the same God who said Thou shalt not bear false witness rewards these midwives just two verses later precisely for bearing false witness.**
- GOOD EGYPTIANS: Israelites did not make up that the bad guys, Egyptians, saved their babies and Moses.
- NAMES OF MIDWIVES: Dennis Prager points out that the name of the Pharoah is never mentioned, but the names of the two lowly midwives are remembered in the Torah for the rest of world history. We usually remember the names of evil people, they make the news. **The Torah is making a powerful point here that goodness is more important than evil.** Here we remember the name of the good midwives, and we do not remember the name of the demigod Pharoah.
- VIGOROUS OR ANIMALS: commentators are split on whether the midwife complimented the Hebrew women or disparaged them. The Hebrew word is chayot, which is the word used in the Old Testament for a wild animal or a wild beast. The wives seem to be saying that the **Hebrew women are just like animals**, like the animals they care for, they give birth out in the field with no one around. It might make the story more convincing to Pharaoh if it is disparaging.
- WHY BOYS? Boys serve in the military, and it was that sort of threat the Pharaoh was worried about.
- WHY BOYS FRACTAL: I don’t normally put much stock in the Jewish Midrash, which I just collections have made up stories filling in the blanks between what’s in the text. However, there is one about why it was just the boys that I found fascinating. **That story said that the pharaohs magicians and astrologers told him that a savior of the Jews was about to be born and that it would be a male.** And so he murdered just the boys to prevent the savior from rising to power. Just like Jesus, except in Jesus‘s case it was the king of Israel, who ordered the babies killed, and Jesus fled to Egypt for safety. A fractal with Moses. The reason I find that fascinating is those are Jews, not Christians, who wrote that story sometime in the range of 800 to 1000 A.D. Even they saw the fractal pattern between Moses‘s life and Jesus life. They just wanted to appropriate it for themselves because Christianity was so powerful in medieval times. **And regardless of whether Pharoah knew a male savior of the Jews was about to be born, he was born. And he survived the slaughter of infants, just as Jesus did** by his parents hiding him somewhere that the evil king would not kill him. Fractal.
- FEARING GOD: why does it say the women didn’t murder the babies? Because they feared God. Is it good to fear God?
  - MODERN CHRISTIANS DOWNPLAY FEAR: How often do you hear in Christian circles about the importance of fearing God? Fear is a favorite bogeyman of the New Age. They claim God is love, and they claim the opposite of love is fear, so fear is the opposite of God. So they tried to teach you to never feel fear. **God would never judge you or punish you, no need to be afraid of that.**
  - DEMONS: Ask yourself, does the enemy (Satan) have any vested interest in getting people not to feel fear? Yes. It is the fear of punishment for our sins that drives us to the cross**.** **We cannot accept Jesus as our savior if we don’t think there’s anything to be saved from.** So the demon who successfully convinces you that God is love and fear is evil is keeping you from the cross.
  - NO FEAR IN LOVE: They will even support it with 1 John 4:18: “There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear. For fear has to do with punishment, and whoever fears has not been perfected in love.” So doesn’t that say don’t fear God, love him instead?
  - HOW TO RECONCILE? “Fear of God” is literally the Torah’s description of a good person, but there is no fear in love and God is love? How do those square with each other? John is saying that **once you’ve made it to the cross through fear,** God will sanctify you and help you learn to trust Jesus more and more and the more you trust him the more you will love him, and the less you will fear that maybe you misunderstood and will get punished after all.
  - FULL FAITH = NO FEAR: So that in the end when you do have full faith that Jesus will save you from the thing you were afraid of that is perfect love. **The fear of punishment that brought you to the cross has turned into trust** that God will do what he said he would do.
  - THE WRONG JESUS: **If you came to Jesus without the fear of punishment for your sins, you came to the wrong Jesus.** The Bible warns us that there will be counterfeit Jesus’s out there, along with false Gospels designed by the enemy to keep you from being saved from your sins by Jesus. If you came to the “no fear all love” Jesus, you missed the point that God cares first and foremost about Good and Evil, and **you have broken God’s law and you need a savior**. The story is not about how good you are and how the loving God recognized your goodness and will now bring you to heaven through the Jesus hot air balloon. It is not about how God just loves you and sin doesn’t matter. No, the story is that you broke God’s law, you have lied and stolen and cheated and so many other sins that all of us have been guilty of. You are the villain, and you CANNOT go to heaven without Jesus BECAUSE God hates sin.
  - GOOD FEAR IS GOOD, BAD FEAR IS BAD: Fear of Pharoah It is how the baby killers are described. This is bad fear. Fear of God is what keeps babies from being murdered. Joseph feared God. These midwives fear God. To fear, God means to refuse evil. “**Fear not him who can kill the body but cannot kill the soul. Rather fear him who can destroy both soul and body in hell.**” Matthew 10:28. These midwives show us that in fact the only hope humanity has to reduce or eliminate evil is widespread fear of God.
  - GOD CARES ABOUT GOOD AND EVIL: so the next time someone tells you that God is only love and fear is bad, just **say to them “Shiphrah and Puah.”** **The two lowly midwives whose names we know while we are left to guess at who the Exodus Pharoah was. Remember those two names.** Because these two ordinary women of extraordinary moral courage deserve to be remembered because they stood up for goodness against the Ubermensch Bureaucracy of Evil.
- WHO DID GOD GIVE FAMILIES TO? The sentence could equivalently be read that because the midwives feared God, many Hebrew boys lived, and God raised up families for them. The very next sentence says that the people multiplied and grew very strong. My gut tells me it was the midwives, but then we do have to wonder why does God reward the midwives who feared God but not the Hebrews who feared God? **It is rare in this life that you see moral courage rewarded by God so clearly.** But if it was the Hebrew boys, all the young boys were thrown in the river later, which doesn’t sound like they had families raised up for them. Both could be true. It is just not clear.

**Exodus 2:1-10 > Moses born**

- NOT A MIRACULOUS BIRTH: Dennis Prager points out that the text almost goes out of its way to make the birth of Moses appear perfectly ordinary. It doesn’t give the names of his mother (Jochebed) and father (Amram), although we do learn later in the story, **it just says a man took a wife and they had a boy.** This is very different than the patriarchs, when God did miraculously lock and unlock the wombs of the matriarchs. It is trying very hard to make sure people don’t worship Moses, they worship God instead. This is not like Jesus, whose birth was miraculous, and he is indeed God in human form.
- SAW HE WAS A FINE CHILD: But in the original Hebrew, there is a clue to the importance of the birth of this child that we do not get in the English. **The birth of Moses is a major turning point in redeeming the world.**
  - CREATION: It is almost like another stage of creation like the end of day 6.25. The actual Hebrew is the exact Hebrew used when God creates the world.
  - TOV: It literally says “And she saw that he was _tov_”, just as God said at the end of each of the first six days of creation: “And God saw that it was _tov_”. We talked at the time, this “good” does not necessarily mean “morally” good (_tzadik_) or perfect (_tamim_). It more accurately means “fit for purpose.” In creation, God said that what he created was “fit for his purpose”, I.e. it would do what he wanted it to do and it would do it well. It was good.
  - “GOOD” BABY? Obviously, Moses’s mother is not saying that he is morally good or perfect. He’s a baby, they are not morally good or bad. They are morally innocent.
  - FIT FOR PURPOSE: No, instead, he is “good” as each day of creation was good, he was “fit for his purpose” in creation. He was the end of day 6 and 1/4, a creation of his mother, and she saw that this creation was good. It may not be a miraculous birth, but it ends up being a miraculous life and will have eternal cosmic consequences on the order of the consequences of creation itself.
- DON’T GLOSS OVER THREE MONTHS: for those three months, Moses’s mother and father made it in to the Hebrews 11 Hall of faith. The greatest examples we have of people who trusted God. “By faith Moses, when he was born, was hidden for three months by his parents, because they saw that the child was beautiful, and they were not afraid of the king's edict.” Hebrews 11:23. **What would the three months have been like?** Terrified, feeding a baby worried about it making sounds. Arguing between wife and husband, but we do with this boy when he is older because all the children his age are dead? How will we hide him forever? Shhh…quiet! Does that sound like a soldier? Will our neighbors who already lost their child turn us in? Who can we trust? Even if he does grow up, is there any life for him but hard slavery in the taskmasters whip? Those three months must have been both awful and awesome, anxiety and love, all mixed together.
- WOMEN ARE THE HEROES: further proof that I don’t think men from ancient cultures made this story up: who are the heroes in the story? Not his father, who is just a certain man from the tribe of Levi. The heroes are the midwives, whose names we remember, Moses‘s mother, Moses‘s sister, the Egyptian princess. **All women.** Who are the villains? The pharaoh, the Egyptian men slave masters. No way men made that up.
- THE SECOND ARK: the first one was Noah’s Ark. We talked at that time that an ark is a vessel with no engine and no route. It is steered, purely by God. Dennis Prager points out that when the first ark appeared, it represented God, saving a family from the fatal punishment for the sins of the world. It represented a new world, a new creation. After the Ark landed, God gave Noah laws for his family and for all humanity. In sort of the same way this ark landing represents the new creation, God saving a family from the fatal consequences of the sin of the world. God will give the occupant of this ark laws for his family and for the entire world. **Fractal.**
- THE DAUGHTER BRINGS THE DOWNFALL: the very thing the pharaoh was afraid of ends up coming to pass through the actions of his daughter and his own lenience toward her keeping this one Hebrew baby. His fear drove him to create a self fulfilling prophecy. **Had he not acted to protect Egypt from the Hebrews in the 1st place, Moses never would have ended up with his daughter.**  As Dennis Prager points out, his daughter is a testament to the fact that just because your parents are evil does not mean you have to be. **You can be the pharaoh’s daughter.**

**Exodus 2:11-15 > Moses kills Egyptian**

- MOSES WAS OF THE TRIBE OF LEVI: Jacob said Levi and Simeon’s anger was devastating. Moses seems to have inherited his ancestor’s anger at unrighteousness.
- WAS HIS KILLING OF THE EGYPTIAN IMMORAL? This is a hard question. We weren’t there, we don’t know what the Egyptian was doing.
  - BEAT = KILL: First, the word in Hebrew used here for beating is the same word for killing. The Egyptian may have been beating the slave with the clear intent to kill him.
  - EGYPTIAN’S ACT = MOSES’ ACT: Dennis Prager pointed out something fascinating that the English translation completely misses. The verb used for what the Egyptian was doing to the Hebrew is the exact same verb for what Moses did to the Egyptian. So if you think the Egyptian was just beating Hebrew instead of killing him, Moses beat the Egyptian and the killing was accidental. If you think the Egyptian was killing the Hebrew slave, then Moses was killing the Egyptian to prevent the killing of the Hebrew slave. **But Moses did not kill him for beating the Hebrew slave.**
  - THERE WAS NO MAN: Also in the Hebrew, it doesn’t say he looked around and seeing no one, it says he looked around, “and there was no man.” This either means he was looking for a man to protect the Hebrew slave so he didn’t have to, or it meant there would be no witness if he intervened. Dennis Prager suggested that it may very well be both: first looking for someone else to do what was right, and then deciding he had to act but he didn’t want witnesses. The Torah’s ambiguity may have been intentional.
  - NO CHOICE BUT TO KILL EGYPTIAN: Once he decided to act, he had no choice but to kill the Egyptian. No one could report this back to the palace or Moses was dead. So his only real options were look away and ignore the evil, or kill the Egyptian. **Not killing the Egyptian meant basically suicide.**
  - LEGAL, NOT MORAL: Regardless, I come down on the side of it was not legal, but it was moral. **Be very wary of judging those who fight evil as one who has never experienced it.** Moses’ killing of the Egyptian is not like killing an entire town for the sins of one man like his ancestor Levi did. The Egyptian died for his own actions, even though his life and job were precisely to perform that sort of action.
  - GOD APPROVES: God seems to approve of Moses’ actions, he chooses Moses to be the greatest Israelite other than Jesus. In fact, Moses prophesies that Jesus will be a prophet like him in Deuteronomy 18:15: "The LORD your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among you, from your brothers-it is to him you shall listen.” **Jesus is a prophet who hates evil enough to kill the Egyptian.** Judgment day is real, and it is terrifying. Jesus offers us a way out, but make no mistake he will kill the Egyptian who does not accept the way out.
  - CHARACTER: Moses’ reaction to this event is one of the great testaments to his character: he hates evil so much he is willing to risk his own life of luxury purely to stop it.
- IN CASE YOU THINK ONLY EGYPTIANS ARE EVIL…: the very next sentence shows us a Hebrew doing exactly the same thing. **The Torah must be the single, most honest historical origin story of any people ever.** Nobody would make this up if it didn’t happen.
- AMBIGUOUS MORALITY WITHOUT GOD’S LAW: the Hebrew’s taunt to Moses is jarring to him, and to us. How could the people he helped betray him like that? Didn’t they see how wrong the Egyptian was? This is the untethering of morality from God we talked about earlier. The Nietzschean superman of Pharaoh and the bureaucracy made it hard for even the victim to see objective good and evil. When the beaten man gets even a tiny amount of power over his fellow man, he wields it violently because it’s all he’s ever known. The beaten become the beaters. But Moses, great man that he was, saw the objective good and evil. And he not only saw it, he had the courage to speak up, driven by a hatred of that objective evil and injustice. Unlike Pharaoh, who uses evil as a means to power. **Moses, the anti-Pharaoh.**
- NATURAL REACTION OF THE GUILTY: Dennis Prager points out that the guilty man’s reaction is normal: he doesn’t address Moses’s question, he knows he is guilty. Instead, he attacks Moses’s own character like “you’re no better than me.” It is actually a complete non-Sequitur to talk about Moses’s past. The correct answer is “I was wrong and I should do better.” The other man can also do better, but the fact that he doesn’t do better is irrelevant to whether you should do better. In your own life, when someone accuses you of something, make sure your first response is not jumping to the sins of your accuser but rather first asking yourself is it true?
- WE NEED GOD’S LAW: are we not still begging God to give his law, to settle the question of good and evil? To tell Moses he was right and tell the Egyptian slave driver he was wrong? To tell the Hebrew he was ungrateful and evil, to treat his brother as he himself wants to be treated?
